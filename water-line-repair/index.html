Water Line Leak: What to Do First

A failed service line can flood a basement or leave the house with no water at all. Shut the main valve inside the house, and if water keeps escaping outside, the town's curb valve may need to be closed. Call the 24/7 line and tell the dispatcher where the line enters and what you see. A line that froze and split is also a burst pipe repair call.

A wet spot in the yard, a gurgling sound, a meter that spins with all fixtures closed and sudden low pressure are the usual signs. Photograph the area and keep people and pets away from any pooling water near electric equipment.

Signs the Service Line Is the Problem

A service line problem affects the whole house, not a single fixture.

  • The meter runs with everything closed: this points to a leak after the meter, often underground.
  • A wet or sunken patch in the lawn: water is surfacing along the line.
  • Low pressure at every fixture: a partly failed line cannot deliver full flow.
  • Dirty or discolored water: a damaged line can let soil in when pressure drops.
  • A bill that rose for no reason: an underground leak can run unseen for months.

Who Owns What: Town and Homeowner

Public water mains are the town's. The line from the main to your house is usually split, with the water department handling the part up to the shutoff near the street and the homeowner responsible for the rest. Ask the Shrewsbury Water Department where your line divides before you start digging, and which side of that point the leak is on.

Low Water Pressure: Is It the Service Line?

Low pressure at every tap can come from a closed valve, a failing pressure reducing valve, rust buildup in old galvanized pipe or a leaking service line. We check the simple causes first. A pressure gauge at an outside hose bib tells us what is arriving at the house before we decide where to look next.

Repair and Replacement Options

The right choice depends on what the leak and the ground allow.

MethodBest forTrade-off
Spot repairOne leak at a known pointDigs one hole
Open-trench replacementFailed or very old lineMost disruptive, most complete
Trenchless replacementLong runs under driveways or landscapingLess digging, needs access pits and a clear path
Interior section repairLeak where the line enters the foundationSmaller work, but access can be tight

Water lines run from the street to the meter and then to the main valve, so a leak search with leak detection often comes first.

Older Pipe, Older Streets

Service lines installed decades ago may be galvanized steel, copper or in some towns lead. Ask the water department what yours is made of. Shrewsbury has older streets, and a line that is old enough to be steel is a candidate for replacement, not repeated repair. Newer lines are often copper or plastic and fail at fittings, not along the pipe.

If you are replacing the line, it is a good time to add a new main shutoff valve inside the house, and sometimes a pressure reducing valve. Other parts of the supply may be worth a look too, and repiping covers the inside piping.

Wells and Private Supply Lines

Not every house in the area is on town water. A home in Boylston on a private well has a well line and a pressure tank in place of a town service line, and the failure points are different. Tell the dispatcher which kind of supply you have so the right plumber and parts come.

What to Do If You Suspect a Buried Leak

Close the main shutoff and watch the meter. Do not dig near the line yourself, because a hit gas, electric or cable line is far more serious than a wet yard.

Service line work follows the state plumbing standards in 248 CMR 10.00, and the town sets its own rules for the connection at the main.
