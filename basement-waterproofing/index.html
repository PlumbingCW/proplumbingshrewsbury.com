Finding Where the Water Comes From

Basement moisture has more than one source. It can be seepage through the walls, water rising through the floor, condensation on cold pipes or a plumbing leak. The fix depends on the source, and a plan built on a guess can waste thousands.

Tape a square of plastic to the wall or floor for a few days. If moisture collects under the plastic, water is coming through the concrete. If it collects on top, the problem is condensation in the air.

Common Wet-Basement Patterns

The pattern points toward the cause.

  • Water at the wall and floor joint after rain: hydrostatic pressure pushing groundwater through the joint.
  • Damp patches on the wall: seepage through a crack or porous block.
  • Water in the middle of the floor: rising groundwater or a hidden plumbing leak.
  • Moisture on pipes and cold walls in summer: condensation, not leaks.
  • Water that appears with no rain: a plumbing leak, which needs leak detection.

Older Foundations in Shrewsbury

Many older houses in Shrewsbury sit on stone or block foundations with joints that can seep, and newer houses have poured concrete that can crack. Neither is wrong, but each moves water differently. We look at the house and the history of water before recommending a system.

Interior drainage and a sump pump are the plumbing side of waterproofing, and they are what we install. Homes in Grafton and other nearby towns with sloping lots often start with grading and gutters.

Why Plumbing Comes First

Before spending on waterproofing, rule out a plumbing leak, a failing water heater or a running line. A damp basement caused by a dripping pipe is a much cheaper fix than a drain system. If you are planning to finish the basement, talk to us about plumbing installation before the walls go up.
