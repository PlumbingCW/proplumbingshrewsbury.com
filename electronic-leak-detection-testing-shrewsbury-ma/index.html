Electronic Leak Detection Testing: Shrewsbury, MA Guide

By the Shrewsbury Pro Plumbing teamShrewsbury, MA homeowner guide

Electronic leak detection finds water leaks behind walls and under floors with sensors, not sledgehammers. Here is how the tools work, what a visit looks like and what happens after the leak is found.

Call (508) 389-4045

Why Electronics Instead of Demolition

A leak inside a wall or under a slab can wet a large area before it shows on the surface. Opening the wrong spot costs money and makes a mess. Electronic tools narrow the search so the opening is small and in the right place.

Detection is a process of elimination. Each tool gives a clue, and together they point to a location.

The Tools and What They Do

No single tool does everything, which is why a technician carries several.

ToolHow it worksBest for
Acoustic listening devicePicks up the hiss of pressurized water escapingSupply lines in walls and slabs
Thermal imaging cameraShows temperature differences from moisture or hot waterWet walls, hot water leaks, radiant lines
Moisture meterMeasures moisture in drywall, wood and flooringConfirming wet areas
Pressure testIsolates a line and watches for a pressure dropConfirming a leak in a section
Tracer gasUses harmless gas to find the leak pathHard-to-locate leaks in sealed lines

What a Detection Visit Looks Like

The technician starts with a conversation. Where did you see the stain, when did the water bill change and which fixtures are on the affected line? Then they check the meter, isolate zones with shutoffs and listen along pipe runs.

Thermal and moisture readings confirm the area, and a pressure test can prove which line is leaking. The goal is a mark on the wall or floor that says the leak is here, not a hole in six places.

Slab Leaks and Hot Water Leaks

A leak under a concrete slab often announces itself with a warm spot on the floor, a sound of running water or an unexplained jump in the hot water bill. Hot water lines show up well on thermal cameras because of the heat they carry.

A slab leak calls for a decision. The line can be repaired by opening the slab, rerouted overhead or replaced, and we explain the options before work starts.

What Detection Cannot Promise

A detection visit narrows the search and gives you a location, but it is not a repair. Very small leaks or leaks in difficult materials can take more than one pass. A good technician tells you how confident they are in the finding.

After the Leak Is Found

Once the leak is pinned down, you have options. A section of pipe can be repaired, a fitting replaced or a long run rerouted. For leaks in the buried line from the street, water service line repair covers the fix.

Tell your insurer promptly. Some policies cover the damage a leak causes but not the repair of the pipe itself, so keep photos, readings and the technician's notes.

Hot Spots, Cold Spots and Water Bills

A warm patch on a floor with no heating pipes underneath suggests a hot water line is leaking. A cold, damp patch under a bathroom suggests a supply or drain leak above it. A water bill that rises month after month without a change in habits points to a leak that runs all the time.

Write down meter readings at the same hour for a few days. A steady climb with no one home is strong evidence for a plumber to start from.

How Long a Search Takes

A simple search in a single room can finish in an hour. A whole-house search with a slab or multiple zones takes longer because each zone has to be isolated and tested. The technician should explain the plan and the limits before beginning.

Ask whether the quote covers the search only or the repair as well. A clear price before work starts avoids confusion on the day.

Leaks in Shrewsbury Homes

Shrewsbury's water supply comes from groundwater wells run by the town's water department, and its meters record your use. A jump in a bill with no change in habits is the best early alarm that a hidden leak exists.

Ask the water department about your account history and meter readings. Their records help a technician date the start of a leak.

Preparing for the Visit

Clear access to the water meter, the main shutoff and the area where you saw signs of water. Note when the problem started, which fixtures run on the affected line and any recent plumbing or renovation work. Photographs of stains with dates are useful.

Keep the house quiet during the visit. Acoustic tools pick up small sounds, and a running dishwasher or a ticking dryer interferes.
