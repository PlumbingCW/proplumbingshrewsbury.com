Basement Taking on Water: What to Do First

If water is rising in the basement, stay out of the room when the electrical panel, outlets or appliances are in the water. Shut off power to the area at the breaker only if you can reach it from a dry spot. Call the 24/7 line, and if the pit is full and the pump is silent, check that the pump is plugged in and the breaker has not tripped.

Move valuables up and off the floor, then photograph the water and the pit for your insurer. A shop vacuum or a temporary pump can slow the rise while the plumber is on the way.

Why Sump Pumps Fail

Most sump failures come down to power, the float or a worn pump.

Installing or Replacing a Sump Pump

A properly installed pump sits in a pit at the lowest point of the basement and discharges through a pipe to the outside, away from the foundation. A check valve on the discharge keeps water from flowing back, and a cover keeps the pit clean. Replacement is usually a matter of an hour or two, and a first-time install includes cutting the pit and running the discharge.

A battery backup or a water-powered backup covers a power loss during a storm, and a water level alarm warns you before the basement floods. We recommend one of the two for a house that has had water in the basement. Homes on the lake shore can read the Lake Quinsigamond page for what is general and what is not.

Test Your Pump Before the Rain

Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch the float lift, the pump run and the water drop. Do it before the wet season and after any power outage. If you live near Lake Quinsigamond or in a low spot, make it a seasonal habit.

Wet Basements Beyond the Pump

A sump pump removes water that has already reached the pit, and it does not stop the water from entering. If the basement is damp even when the pump works, grading, gutters, cracks or interior drains may be needed, and basement waterproofing explains the options. A hidden plumbing leak can also look like a wet basement, which leak detection can rule out.
