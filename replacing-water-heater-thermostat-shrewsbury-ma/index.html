Replacing a Water Heater Thermostat in Shrewsbury, MA

By the Shrewsbury Pro Plumbing teamShrewsbury, MA homeowner guide

A water heater that runs cold, runs lukewarm or runs too hot often has a bad thermostat, not a bad tank. This guide shows how to tell, how the swap works on an electric unit, and when to stop and call.

Call (508) 389-4045

What the Thermostat Does and Why It Fails

An electric tank water heater usually has two thermostats, one near the top of the tank and one near the bottom. Each one switches an electric heating element on when the water cools and off when it reaches the set temperature. When a thermostat sticks, drifts or burns out, the element either never turns on or never turns off.

Thermostats fail from heat cycling, loose wiring and moisture getting into the access panel. A unit that has run for a decade or more has simply cycled thousands of times. That wear is normal, and a thermostat is a modest part compared with a whole tank.

How the Swap Works on an Electric Tank

This is a general outline, and the label on your tank and the part instructions come first.

  1. 1Turn the power off at the breakerSwitch it off at the panel, then confirm the wires are dead with a non-contact tester before you touch anything.
  2. 2Remove the access panel and insulationTake off the cover plate and move the insulation aside. Keep the plastic safety guard in mind, because it sits over the live terminals.
  3. 3Photograph the wiringTake a clear picture of the wires and where each one connects. A mistake here is the usual reason a swap goes wrong.
  4. 4Disconnect and remove the old thermostatLabel the wires, pull them off, and slide the thermostat out of its clips.
  5. 5Fit the matching new partUse a thermostat rated for the tank. Press it flat against the tank wall so it reads the temperature correctly.
  6. 6Reconnect, close up and restore powerWire it as photographed, replace the guard, insulation and cover, and only then turn the breaker back on. Fill the tank first if you drained it.

When to Stop and Call a Plumber

Gas water heaters do not have the same kind of thermostat. They use a gas control valve with its own thermostat built in, and that job involves gas and venting, so leave it to a licensed professional. A tank that leaks from the shell, a temperature relief valve that keeps dripping or a breaker that trips again after a new part are all signs the problem is bigger than a thermostat.

If you are not comfortable working near live wiring, that is a good enough reason to call. Our water heater repair page explains what a visit covers, and water heater replacement covers the case where the tank itself has failed.

Setting the Temperature Once It Works

Check the temperature label on the tank or the manufacturer's manual for the recommended setting. Hotter water raises scald risk, especially in a home with children or older adults, and it also shortens the life of the heater. A sensible setting balances comfort, safety and wear.

After the repair, run a hot tap for a few minutes and feel the difference. If the water is still lukewarm after an hour, the lower element or the dip tube may be the next suspect.

Gas Versus Electric: Know Which You Own

Check the label on the tank before you buy any part. An electric heater has two wires running to the top and a metal access panel on the side. A gas heater has a burner at the bottom, a flue pipe out the top and a gas control valve with a dial on the front.

The advice in this article applies to electric tanks only. If yours is a gas unit and the water is not heating, the pilot, the thermocouple or the gas control valve are the likelier suspects, and those repairs involve fuel and venting.

Shrewsbury Water and Your Heater

Shrewsbury's public water comes from groundwater wells run by the town's water department, and well water carries dissolved minerals. Minerals settle at the bottom of a tank over the years and can coat the lower element, which makes it work harder and fail sooner.

The department publishes a yearly water quality report, so ask it for the latest one, or test your tap, before you decide how often to flush the tank. A lower element buried in sediment is a common reason a lower thermostat seems to fail.
