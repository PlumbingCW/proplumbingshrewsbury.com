Sewer Backup: What to Do First

A drain that backs up again after a snaking is an emergency for anyone with a finished basement. Call the 24/7 line, stop using water fixtures and keep everyone out of the wet area, since backup water carries bacteria. Tell the dispatcher what the last plumber tried, because a cable that stopped at the same spot twice is the classic reason to jet.

What Hydro Jetting Actually Does

A jetter pushes a hose with a forward and rear-facing nozzle into the drain and runs water at very high pressure through it. The rear jets pull the hose forward while the spray scrubs the entire pipe wall. Grease, soap scum, mineral scale and small roots wash out toward the cleanout instead of being punched through in one hole.

That difference is why jetting lasts longer on a grease-coated line. A cable cuts a channel the width of the cutter head. A jetter cleans the full diameter, which restores the flow the pipe was built to carry.

Where Jetting Pays Off in Shrewsbury

Restaurants and food shops along the Boston Turnpike send fats and starches into their drains every day, and those lines build up faster than a house line does. Commercial plumbing customers often ask for scheduled jetting for that reason, and the same crews work the Route 9 corridor into Westborough. A kitchen line serving a busy household can build up the same way over a few years.

Older homes with cast iron drains benefit too. Scale on the inside of the pipe catches grease and paper, so jetting clears the pipe and the surface that was catching waste. Roots that have entered joints are cut first, then the line is flushed clean.

Jetting Needs a Camera Before and After

A jetter can make a damaged pipe worse, so we look inside before we turn up the pressure and again afterward. The second look confirms the pipe is clean and shows any cracks, offsets or low spots the dirt was hiding. A sewer camera inspection is the usual way to do both.
